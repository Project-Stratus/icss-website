Project Stratus

AI-navigated high altitude ballooning for environmental monitoring

Project Stratus builds and launches high altitude weather balloons. We're adding release valves, ballast and an AI agent so that the balloon can move up and down to catch winds blowing in different directions. The idea is that it can then stay roughly in one place (within about 100 km of the launch site) rather than drifting off, and that we can recover it rather than letting it burst.

If this works, it gives a cheap alternative to low orbit satellites for weather monitoring and Earth observation. The long-term ambition is an affordable balloon platform capable of multi-day atmospheric observations.

Stratus flight, 7 February 2026

What we did last year

The big milestone this year was the successful launch of a helium-filled latex balloon to approximately 18,000 metres, which also collected temperature and pressure data on the way. We also designed a payload enclosure and an ultrasonic anemometer (for measuring wind speed), and wrote a preliminary reinforcement-learning controller to explore how changing altitude could keep the balloon within a target area.

This year

  • develop and test a valve and ballast system
  • demonstrate autonomous altitude control
  • improve the reinforcement-learning model
  • improve the reliability of the payload

The AI bit

The balloon can only control its height, so the reinforcement-learning model has to learn which altitude to sit at in order to ride the right wind. So far we have a basic 3D atmospheric environment and a fully trained 1D agent. The next steps are to include real-world limitations (ballast, helium etc.) in the model and to expand it to more dimensions.

On board

The payload carries a temperature sensor, barometer, battery monitor and GPS (all over I2C), a microSD card and LoRaWAN antenna (over serial) and a UV sensor (analog), as well as a camera, power bank, insulated container and parachute. All of our code is on our GitHub.

We're also looking into detecting "ship tracks" (clouds formed from ship exhaust) to help with the monitoring and regulation of fuel sulphur content.

What you'll learn

You'd be doing a mix of electronics and soldering, radio and GPS work, reinforcement learning, and regular trips to the Advanced Hackspace (lathe, waterjet cutter, TIG welding) to make the valve. Then you get to see it all tested on a real flight, 18 km up, where it's around -55°C and the air is less than a tenth as thick as on the ground.
